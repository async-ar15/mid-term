# THE MASTER PICTURE: SUSTAINABILITY & CLIMATE CHANGE

**The Fundamental Problem:**
- Human societies take resources from the Earth, transform them into economic and social value, and return waste and pollution to the Earth.
- The problem begins when the rate of extraction and pollution exceeds the rate at which natural systems can regenerate, absorb, or recover.

**The Master Chain:**
- Human activity → resource extraction & emissions → environmental change → climate/ecological impacts → social & economic consequences → need for sustainable development → measurement → policy/markets → technological & operational solutions.

# UNIT 1 — THE SCIENCE OF CLIMATE CHANGE

## 1.1 Key Distinctions

### Weather vs Climate vs Climate Change vs Global Warming
- **Weather** — what the atmosphere is doing over a short period at a particular place.
- **Climate** — the long-term pattern of weather (statistical averages over 30+ years).
- **Climate Change** — the broader long-term change in the climate system.
- **Global Warming** — specifically refers to the long-term increase in average global temperature.

### Two Definitions of Climate Change
- **IPCC definition** — any change in climate over time, whether due to natural variability or human activity.
- **UNFCCC definition** — a change of climate attributed directly or indirectly to human activity that alters the composition of the global atmosphere, observed over comparable time periods (policy-oriented, emphasizes human influence).

---

## 1.2 Earth as a System

### Five Components of the Climate System
- **Atmosphere** — gases surrounding Earth; contains greenhouse gases.
- **Hydrosphere** — oceans, rivers, lakes; the ocean acts as a huge heat buffer because it absorbs and releases heat slowly.
- **Cryosphere** — ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, permafrost; responsible for the albedo effect (reflecting sunlight).
- **Lithosphere** — rocks, soil, sediments; stores carbon in geological formations.
- **Biosphere** — all living organisms; forests act as carbon sinks.

### System Behaviour
- These five components continuously exchange heat, water, and carbon.
- Think of it like a distributed system: different planetary "nodes" interact.
- Feedback loops can amplify changes.
- The ocean acts as a huge buffer because it absorbs and releases heat slowly.

---

## 1.3 Earth's Energy Balance

### How It Works
- **Incoming solar radiation** provides energy to Earth.
- Some sunlight is **reflected** back into space by clouds, ice, and bright surfaces — this is the **albedo effect**.
- The remainder is **absorbed** by the Earth's surface.
- The Earth then **re-emits** this energy as infrared (longwave) radiation.

### The Natural Greenhouse Effect
- Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere **absorb** some of this outgoing infrared energy.
- They then **re-radiate** it in all directions, including back toward the surface.
- This is essential for life: **without it**, Earth's average temperature would be about **−18°C**.
- **With it**, Earth's average temperature is roughly **+15°C**.

### The Problem
- The problem is **not** the greenhouse effect itself.
- The problem is the **human amplification** of it — adding more greenhouse gases, trapping more heat, creating an energy imbalance.

---

## 1.4 Greenhouse Gases

### Major Greenhouse Gases
- **CO₂ (Carbon Dioxide)** — dominates because humans emit enormous quantities and it has a long atmospheric lifetime. Pre-industrial level was ~280 ppm; now exceeds 420 ppm.
- **CH₄ (Methane)** — much more potent per molecule than CO₂ but shorter-lived. Sources include agriculture, wetlands, and fossil fuels.
- **N₂O (Nitrous Oxide)** — even more potent than methane. Sources include agriculture and industrial processes.
- **SF₆ (Sulfur Hexafluoride)** — extremely high global warming potential; used in industrial applications.

### Global Warming Potential (GWP)
- GWP allows different gases to be converted into a **common CO₂-equivalent unit**.
- It measures how much heat a gas traps relative to CO₂ over a specified time horizon.
- This is why emissions are reported in **tCO₂e** (tonnes of CO₂ equivalent).

---

## 1.5 Evidence of Warming

### Independent Evidence Streams
- **Thermometer records** — direct temperature measurements.
- **Ice cores** — extend the atmospheric record back ~800,000 years; trapped air bubbles show past CO₂ levels.
- **Keeling Curve** — continuous atmospheric CO₂ measurements at Mauna Loa since 1958; shows relentless long-term increase.
- **Satellites** — global temperature monitoring from space.
- **Glacier observations** — worldwide retreat documented.
- **Sea-level measurements** — rising due to thermal expansion and ice melt.
- **Tree rings and corals** — proxy data providing independent confirmation.

### The Core Reasoning
- More greenhouse gases → more outgoing heat retained → energy imbalance → warming → changes throughout the climate system.

---

## 1.6 Human Causes of Climate Change

### Major Human Sources
- **Fossil-fuel combustion** — coal, oil, natural gas for energy, transport, and industry.
- **Deforestation** — releases stored carbon AND destroys future carbon sinks.
- **Industrial activity** — cement, steel, chemicals production.
- **Agriculture** — methane from livestock and rice paddies; N₂O from fertilizers.
- **Land-use change** — converting forests and wetlands.

### Key Fact
- Human activities have been the **dominant driver** of observed warming since around **1950**.
- Natural factors (solar variability, volcanic activity) exist but are **insufficient** to explain the observed warming.

---

## 1.7 The Carbon Cycle

### Fast Carbon Cycle
- Operates over **days to centuries**.
- Carbon moves between atmosphere, biosphere (plants), and ocean surface.
- Processes: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition.

### Slow Geological Carbon Cycle
- Operates over **thousands to millions of years**.
- Involves rock weathering, sedimentation, and fossil fuel formation.
- Carbon locked away safely in geological deposits.

### Human Disruption
- Humans have taken carbon that was locked in the **slow cycle** for millions of years.
- We have **rapidly injected** it into the **fast cycle** through fossil-fuel combustion.
- Deforestation simultaneously **releases** stored carbon and **destroys** future carbon sinks.

### Planetary Accounting View
- **Natural system**: sources ≈ sinks (balanced).
- **Human disruption**: additional sources > natural ability of sinks to absorb them.
- **Result**: atmospheric CO₂ rises → greenhouse effect strengthens → warming increases.

---

## 1.8 Feedback Loops

### Ice-Albedo Positive Feedback
- Warming → ice melts → less sunlight reflected → more solar energy absorbed → **more warming**.

### Permafrost Positive Feedback
- Warming → permafrost thaws → methane and CO₂ released → **more warming** → more thawing.

### Water Vapour Positive Feedback
- Warming → more evaporation → water vapour is a GHG → **more warming**.

### Why Feedbacks Matter
- They make climate change a **nonlinear systems problem**.
- Small changes can trigger **cascading effects**.
- This is why the course connects climate science to computer science, modelling, and AI.

## 1.9 Impacts of Climate Change

### Environmental Impacts
- Sea-level rise
- Extreme weather events (floods, storms, heatwaves)
- Biodiversity loss
- Ocean acidification
- Wildfires

### Human System Impacts
- **Food security** — heat, drought, and rainfall changes affect agriculture and crop yields.
- **Water security** — drought and flooding disrupt water supply.
- **Migration** — climate refugees displaced by environmental change.
- **Public health** — heat stress, disease vectors, pollution, food and water insecurity.
- **Air quality** — degradation from wildfires and pollution.

### Sector-Specific Risks
- **Agriculture** — yield loss, pest changes.
- **Water** — supply disruption.
- **Infrastructure** — damage from extreme events.
- **Energy** — demand shifts, supply vulnerability.
- **Tourism** — ecosystem degradation.
- **Insurance** — rising claims.
- **Finance** — stranded assets.
- **Digital infrastructure** — data centre cooling challenges.

## 1.10 Mitigation vs Adaptation

### Mitigation — Reduce the Cause
- Solar/wind replacing fossil electricity.
- Energy efficiency improvements.
- Carbon tax as a financial incentive.
- Reforestation and afforestation.
- Methane capture.
- Electric vehicles.

### Adaptation — Manage the Consequences
- Flood early-warning systems.
- Drought-resistant crops.
- Relocating vulnerable infrastructure.
- Sea walls and coastal defences.
- Heat action plans.
- Climate-resilient agriculture.

### Why Both Are Necessary
- Some climate change is **already locked into the system**.
- Mitigation reduces **future** warming.
- Adaptation manages **current and near-term** impacts.

## 1.11 International Governance

### Historical Progression
- **Stockholm 1972** — first global environmental conference; foundation of UNEP.
- **UNFCCC 1992** — Framework Convention on Climate Change; established principles including Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR).
- **Kyoto Protocol 1997** — first legally binding emission targets for **developed countries** (Annex I); introduced carbon trading mechanisms (CDM, JI, Emissions Trading).
- **Paris Agreement 2015** — nearly universal framework; based on **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)**; goal of holding warming **well below 2°C**, pursuing efforts toward **1.5°C**; countries periodically submit stronger commitments; **Global Stocktake** assesses collective progress.
- **COP26 & India's Panchamrit** — India's five commitments:
  - 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030
  - 50% energy needs from renewables by 2030
  - 1 billion tonne CO₂ reduction by 2030
  - Net-zero target by **2070**

## 1.12 MRV — The Hidden Requirement

### What Is MRV?
- **Measurement** — quantify actual emissions (sensors, satellites, IoT).
- **Reporting** — standardized frameworks, national inventories, corporate disclosures.
- **Verification** — independent third-party checks to ensure credibility.

### Why It Matters
- A country **cannot credibly claim** emissions reductions unless it can measure them, report them, and have them verified.
- No credible climate promise exists without MRV.

### Technology Enablers
- Software and databases
- Blockchain for immutable records
- AI for pattern detection and fraud detection
- Remote sensing and satellites

### Key Connection
- This is where climate science connects to **computer science**.

# UNIT 2 — WHAT DOES "SUSTAINABILITY" ACTUALLY MEAN?

## 2.1 Sustainable Development

### The Brundtland Definition
- Development that **meets the needs of the present** without compromising the ability of **future generations** to meet their own needs.

### Two Hidden Ideas in the Definition
- **Needs** — the essential needs of the world's poor receive particular priority.
- **Limitations** — environmental limitations depend partly on technology and social organization.

### Key Insight
- Sustainable development is **not anti-development**.
- It is development that **respects ecological limits**.

---

## 2.2 Intergenerational Equity

### The Core Question
- Future generations **cannot vote**, cannot participate in today's decisions, and cannot defend their interests.
- How do today's decisions account for them?

### Edith Brown Weiss — Three Principles
- **Conservation of Options** — don't destroy the range of resources and choices available to future generations.
- **Conservation of Quality** — pass the environment forward in **no worse condition**.
- **Conservation of Access** — preserve **equitable access** to the environmental legacy.

### Intergenerational vs Intragenerational Equity
- **Intergenerational** — fairness between present and future generations.
- **Intragenerational** — fairness among people living **today** (North-South divide, rich vs poor within countries).

### The Discount Rate Dilemma
- The same **$1 million** benefit 100 years from now is valued at:
  - ~**$249,000** at a 1.4% discount rate
  - ~**$7,600** at a 5% discount rate
- A seemingly **technical economic parameter** contains an **ethical judgment** about how much today's society values future generations.

---

## 2.3 Triple Bottom Line (TBL)

### Three Pillars
- **People** — social outcomes.
- **Planet** — environmental outcomes.
- **Profit** — economic viability.

### John Elkington's Idea
- Organizational success should **not** be judged purely by financial profit.
- Economic, social, and environmental outcomes must **all** be considered.

### Important Criticism
- TBL can become merely a **reporting checklist**.
- Companies may measure social and environmental indicators **without changing** the underlying business model.
- Risk of surface-level compliance.

### Summary Framework
- **Sustainable Development** = the destination.
- **Intergenerational Equity** = the ethical reason.
- **Triple Bottom Line** = the organizational scorecard.

---

## 2.4 The Four Practical Tests of Sustainability

### Test 1: Ecological Integrity
- Can ecosystems remain **functional and resilient**?
- Requires maintaining ecosystem **composition** (species diversity), **structure** (physical organization), **function** (nutrient cycling, energy flow), and **resilience** (ability to recover from disturbance).

#### Planetary Boundaries Framework
- Provides a scientific framework for the **limits** within which human systems operate.
- **Nine planetary boundaries** identified.
- **Six transgressed** as of the 2023 Richardson et al. assessment.
- Defines the **safe operating space** for humanity.

---

### Test 2: Resource Efficiency
- Can we create the **same value** using **fewer resources**?
- Leads to the idea of **decoupling** economic growth from resource consumption.
  - **Relative decoupling** — resource intensity falls, but total resource use still rises.
  - **Absolute decoupling** — total resource use actually falls.

#### The IPAT Equation
- **I = P × A × T**
  - I = Environmental Impact
  - P = Population
  - A = Affluence (consumption per person)
  - T = Technology (environmental intensity per unit of consumption)

#### IPAT Example
- Population increases 10%, affluence increases 20%, technology intensity falls 25%:
  - 1.10 × 1.20 × 0.75 = **0.99**
  - Total impact remains approximately unchanged despite population and economic growth.

#### Jevons Paradox — CRITICAL WARNING
- Efficiency can reduce resource use **per unit**.
- But it makes the resource **cheaper** and therefore more attractive to use.
- Total consumption can actually **increase**.
- **Efficiency alone does not guarantee sustainability.**
- This is why **hard limits, pricing mechanisms, and behavioural/system changes** matter alongside efficiency.

---

### Test 3: Social Equity
- Are benefits and burdens **distributed fairly**?
- Sustainability isn't achieved if a project is efficient and profitable but places pollution or displacement disproportionately on **communities with less power**.
- **Distributive justice** — fair allocation of resources and impacts.
- **Procedural justice** — fair processes in decision-making; affected communities have a voice.

---

### Test 4: Economic Viability
- Can the activity **continue** once its **real costs** are included?
- Introduces the concept of **externalities** — costs imposed on society that don't appear in the market price.

#### Externality Example
- A product costs ₹100 to buy but creates ₹30 of unpriced pollution and health damage.
- **True Social Cost** = Market Price + Externalized Cost = ₹100 + ₹30 = **₹130**.
- This is the basic economic argument behind **environmental taxation** and **carbon pricing**.

---

## 2.6 Carbon Accounting

### Core Principle
- You **cannot manage** what you **cannot measure**.
- A carbon footprint converts different greenhouse gases into a common unit: **tCO₂e** (tonnes of CO₂ equivalent).

### The Formula
- **Emissions = Activity Data × Emission Factor × GWP**

### Three Scopes

#### Scope 1 — Direct Emissions
- Sources **owned or controlled** by the organization.
- Generators, boilers, company vehicles, fugitive refrigerants, process emissions.

#### Scope 2 — Purchased Energy Emissions
- **Indirect** emissions from purchased electricity, steam, heating, or cooling.

#### Scope 3 — Value-Chain Emissions
- **Everything else** upstream and downstream.
- Purchased materials and services, transportation, business travel, employee commuting, product use, end-of-life treatment, investments.

### GreenTech Case Study
- Scope 1: **120 tCO₂e**
- Scope 2: **1,454 tCO₂e**
- Scope 3: **~8,500 tCO₂e**
- Total: **10,074 tCO₂e**
- **Scope 3 = 84% of the total footprint.**

### Crucial Insight
- Scope 3 is often the **largest** part of a company's footprint.
- A company focusing only on Scope 1 and 2 could **miss most of its actual climate impact**.
- This is why you must go into the **supply chain** — which connects directly to Unit 3.

## 2.7 Carbon Pricing and Carbon Markets

### Economic Foundation
- Pollution creates costs for society that may not appear in the producer's market price — these are **externalities**.
- Carbon pricing asks: **What happens if emitting has a financial cost?**
- Goal: **internalize the externality**.

### Two Fundamental Mechanisms

#### Carbon Tax
- Government **fixes the price** of carbon.
- Resulting emissions quantity is **uncertain**.
- Simple to implement.
- Revenue can fund green transition.

#### Cap-and-Trade
- Government **fixes the total quantity** of allowed emissions (the cap).
- The **market determines the price**.
- Process:
  1. Set cap on total emissions.
  2. Distribute allowances.
  3. Companies trade allowances.
  4. Companies surrender allowances for actual emissions.
- An **allowance** = a legal permission to emit one tonne of CO₂e.

### Allowance vs Credit — CRITICAL DISTINCTION
- **Allowance** — permission to emit; part of a compliance system; created by government.
- **Carbon Credit/Offset** — represents a claimed reduction or removal **elsewhere**; can be in voluntary or compliance markets.
- This distinction is **extremely important for exams**.

### Four Credibility Tests for Voluntary Credits
- **Additionality** — the reduction would **not have happened** without the credit incentive.
- **Permanence** — the reduction/removal remains valid **over time** (e.g., a forest could burn down).
- **Verification** — independent verification confirms the claimed reduction.
- **No Double Counting** — the same reduction cannot be claimed **multiple times**.

### Verra Case
- A credit can exist in a registry and be "verified" under a methodology.
- But questions can remain about whether the claimed climate benefit is **genuinely additional** or **accurately quantified**.

### India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)
- Unlike an absolute emissions cap, uses **emissions-intensity targets** (emissions per unit of output).
- An organization that performs **better** than its target → earns **Carbon Credit Certificates**.
- An organization that **underperforms** → must **buy** certificates.
- **Rationale**: India wants industrial production and economic development to continue while making each unit of production **progressively cleaner**.

### Carbon Markets Logic Chain
- Measurement → Target → Financial Incentive → Behavioural/Technological Change → Verification.
- Technology role: MRV, satellites, registries, blockchain, AI-based fraud detection.

# UNIT 3 — HOW ORGANIZATIONS ACTUALLY REDUCE IMPACTS

### The Central Question
- We know the environmental problem, we can measure it and price it. But **where does a company actually change its behaviour**?
- Answer: **Across the entire value chain.**

### Product Lifecycle
- Raw materials → sourcing → production → transportation → distribution → customer use → end-of-life.
- Sustainability must follow the product through **the entire chain**.

---

## 3.1 Sustainable Sourcing

### Core Question
- Where did our materials come from?
- What environmental and social consequences occurred **before they reached us**?

### Issues Beyond Carbon
- Labour conditions
- Deforestation
- Biodiversity destruction
- Conflict minerals
- Human rights violations
- Resource extraction impacts

### Certifications and Auditing
- **Fairtrade** — labour rights and fair-price considerations.
- **FSC (Forest Stewardship Council)** — responsible forestry and chain-of-custody tracking.
- **Rainforest Alliance** — environmental, social, and economic sustainability of agricultural supply chains.
- **Responsible Minerals Initiative** — responsible sourcing of minerals.

### Rana Plaza Case (2013)
- Building collapse in Bangladesh killed **1,134 people**.
- Global buyers lacked adequate visibility and oversight over suppliers.
- Became a major reference point for **supply-chain auditing** and **worker safety**.

### Supplier Tiers
- **Tier 1** — your direct supplier (highest visibility and control).
- **Tier 2** — supplies your Tier 1 supplier (less visibility).
- **Tier 3** — may involve raw material extraction (least visibility and control).

### Key Challenge
- As you move upstream, **contractual control declines** and **data visibility declines**.
- This is one of the **biggest practical challenges** in Scope 3 accounting.

---

## 3.2 Sustainable Production and Life-Cycle Thinking

### Production Tools
- Energy efficiency in manufacturing
- Water efficiency and recycling
- Waste minimization
- Safer chemical substitution
- Process optimization
- Material substitution

### Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)
- Prevents us from looking at **only one stage** of a product's life.
- Provides a **holistic** environmental impact view.

### Three LCA Boundaries
- **Cradle-to-Gate** — raw material extraction through production/factory gate (excludes use and disposal).
- **Cradle-to-Grave** — extraction through production, use, and disposal (full lifecycle, but linear).
- **Cradle-to-Cradle** — the product is designed so its materials become **inputs into another cycle** (connects to circular economy).

### Material Substitution Example
- **Virgin plastic**: 0.050 kg × 3.5 kgCO₂e/kg = **0.175 kgCO₂e/unit**
- **Recycled plastic**: 0.050 kg × 1.2 kgCO₂e/kg = **0.060 kgCO₂e/unit**
- **Saving**: 0.115 kgCO₂e/unit
- At **1 million units**: 115,000 kg = **115 tonnes CO₂e saved per year**.

### Key Lesson
- Sustainability doesn't always require redesigning the entire product.
- Sometimes **changing the material input** alone can substantially reduce embodied emissions.

---

## 3.3 Sustainable Distribution

### Central Idea
- Same product + same distance + same quantity ≠ same environmental impact.
- **Transportation mode matters enormously.**

### Transport Emissions Example
- 20-tonne shipment travelling 6,000 km:
  - **Sea**: 20 × 6,000 × 10 ÷ 1,000 = **1.2 tonnes CO₂e**
  - **Air**: 20 × 6,000 × 600 ÷ 1,000 = **72 tonnes CO₂e**
  - Air creates about **60× more emissions** than sea.

### Distribution Levers
- **Modal shift** — air/road → rail/sea where feasible (biggest single reduction opportunity).
- **Route optimization** — reduce distance and eliminate empty return trips.
- **Packaging reduction** — reduce material and shipping weight.
- **Warehouse efficiency** — improve location and energy use.
- **Reverse logistics** — returns, repairs, and end-of-life collection.

### Win-Win Insight
- Sustainability can sometimes produce **both environmental and economic benefits** (lower fuel costs, less waste, better routes).

---

## 3.4 Resource Management

### Energy

#### Efficiency vs Conservation
- **Efficiency** — same service, less energy (e.g., LED replaces bulb → same light, less power).
- **Conservation** — reduces the service (e.g., turning off a light → no light).

#### Application Areas
- Buildings and HVAC
- Industrial processes
- Lighting systems
- Transportation

---

### Water

#### Context
- Significant **water stress**, especially in India.

#### Interventions
- Low-flow fixtures
- Rainwater harvesting
- Wastewater recycling
- Precision irrigation

#### Rainwater Harvesting Formula
- **Harvestable Water = Area × Rainfall × Runoff Coefficient × 1000**
- Example: 500 m² roof, 900 mm rainfall, 0.85 runoff coefficient:
  - 500 × 0.9 × 0.85 × 1000 = **382,500 litres/year**

---

### Waste Management

#### Waste Hierarchy (Top to Bottom)
1. **Refuse/Reduce** — best option
2. **Reuse**
3. **Recycle**
4. **Recover** — energy recovery
5. **Dispose** — last resort

#### Philosophy
- The best waste is **waste that was never created**.
- Landfill is the **last option**, not the first.

#### Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)
- Pushes **end-of-life responsibility** back onto producers.
- Creates an incentive to design products that are easier to collect, reuse, and recycle.
- Closes the loop.

---

## 3.5 Circular Economy

### Linear vs Circular

#### Linear (Traditional)
- Take → Make → Use → **Dispose**.
- Extraction-based, waste-generating.

#### Circular (New Model)
- Design → Use → Reuse/Repair/Remanufacture/Recycle → **Keep materials circulating**.
- Preserve value, prevent extraction.

### Ellen MacArthur Framework — Three Core Principles
1. **Design out waste and pollution** — eliminate waste at the design stage, not manage it after creation.
2. **Keep products and materials in use** — extend product life; share, repair, remanufacture.
3. **Regenerate natural systems** — return biological nutrients safely; enhance rather than degrade ecosystems.

### Two Major Loops
- **Technical cycle** — metals, plastics, electronics, glass remain in **industrial circulation** (maintain, reuse, remanufacture, recycle).
- **Biological cycle** — food, wood, natural fibres, organic materials return safely to **natural systems** (composting, anaerobic digestion, regenerating soil and ecosystems).

### Critical Insight
- Circular economy is **NOT merely "recycling"**.
- Recycling is only **one part** of a much larger system.
- The objective is to **preserve value** and **prevent extraction** in the first place.
- It is **system redesign**, not just waste management.
