# 3. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Welcome to Module 3! GANs are one of the most powerful and popular frameworks in Generative AI. Instead of explicitly modeling the probability density function like VAEs, GANs use an adversarial game between two neural networks to learn the data distribution implicitly.

---

## 1. Adversarial Training & Architecture

A GAN consists of two neural networks playing a continuous game of cat-and-mouse:
1. **Generator ($G$):** Takes a random noise vector $z$ (sampled from a simple prior like a Gaussian) and tries to generate a fake image $\hat{x} = G(z)$ that looks as real as possible. Its goal is to fool the discriminator.
2. **Discriminator ($D$):** Acts as a binary classifier. It takes an image as input (either a real image $x$ from the dataset or a fake image $G(z)$ from the generator) and outputs a probability $D(x) \in [0, 1]$ indicating how likely the image is real.

### The Min-Max Optimization Problem
The training process is formulated as a **minimax game**. 
* The Discriminator wants to **maximize** its ability to correctly classify real vs. fake.
* The Generator wants to **minimize** the Discriminator's success rate.

The combined value function $V(G, D)$ is:
$$\min_G \max_D V(G, D) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}[\log (1 - D(G(z)))]$$

---

## 2. Loss Functions

### Discriminator Loss ($L_D$)
The Discriminator is trained using standard Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss. Since we want $D$ to maximize the value function, minimizing the loss means minimizing the negative of the value function:
$$L_D = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log D(x_i) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log(1 - D(G(z_i)))$$
* Term 1 pushes $D(x)$ close to $1$ (real images).
* Term 2 pushes $D(G(z))$ close to $0$ (fake images).

### Generator Loss ($L_G$)
According to the original minimax formulation, the generator tries to minimize:
$$L_G = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log(1 - D(G(z_i)))$$
*Note: In practice, this leads to vanishing gradients when the discriminator is too good early in training. Therefore, a "non-saturating" heuristic loss is often used where $G$ instead maximizes $\log D(G(z))$, but exams usually stick to the theoretical minimax definition unless specified.*

### The Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) Connection
If the discriminator $D$ is trained to its absolute theoretical optimum $D^*_G$ for a fixed generator, the minimax objective reduces mathematically to:
$$V(G, D^*_G) = 2 \cdot D_{JSD}(p_{data} || p_g) - \log 4$$
This means that optimizing the Generator is mathematically equivalent to **minimizing the Jensen-Shannon Divergence** between the real data distribution and the generated data distribution!

---

## 3. Training Challenges

Working with GANs is notoriously difficult in practice.
### 1. Non-Convergence / Unstable Optimization
Because GANs involve two networks competing against each other, there is no single global loss function being minimized. Instead of finding a global minimum, they are looking for a **Nash Equilibrium**. If one network becomes too strong too quickly, the gradients vanish or explode, causing the loss values to oscillate wildly without ever converging.

### 2. Mode Collapse
This happens when the Generator figures out a single, specific output (a "mode") that reliably fools the Discriminator. Instead of learning the entire diverse distribution of the dataset, the Generator becomes lazy and produces the exact same image (or a tiny handful of images) over and over again, regardless of the input noise $z$. The Discriminator gets stuck in a loop trying to reject that specific image, and diversity is lost entirely.

---

## 4. Evaluation Metrics

Because GANs don't have a tractable likelihood function like VAEs, we cannot easily evaluate them using probability scores. Instead, we use classifiers (like a pre-trained Inception Network) to evaluate sample quality. A good generator must produce samples that have both **Sharpness** (high quality, looks like a distinct object) and **Diversity** (covers all classes in the dataset).

* **Inception Score (IS):**
  * **Sharpness:** Evaluated by looking at $P(y|x)$ (the classifier's confidence). A sharp image will confidently trigger a single class.
  * **Diversity:** Evaluated by looking at $P(y)$ (the marginal distribution). A diverse generator will trigger all classes uniformly over a large batch.
  * IS combines these using KL-Divergence. **Higher IS is better.**
* **Frechet Inception Distance (FID):**
  * IS only looks at generated images. FID compares the generated images directly to the real test images.
  * It extracts feature vectors from an intermediate layer of the Inception Network for both real and fake images.
  * It fits a multivariate Gaussian to both feature sets and calculates the Frechet Distance between them.
  * **Lower FID is better** (0 means the distributions match perfectly).
* **Kernel Inception Distance (KID):**
  * Similar to FID but uses Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) instead of fitting Gaussians. It is unbiased and works better on smaller batch sizes.

---

## 5. GAN Variants

To combat training instability and mode collapse, several architectural variants have been introduced:

* **Conditional GAN (cGAN):** Instead of generating random images, we feed a class label $y$ (or text condition) into BOTH the Generator and Discriminator. This forces the Generator to learn the specific modes associated with each label, heavily mitigating mode collapse and allowing user-controlled generation (e.g., Text-to-Image).
* **Wasserstein GAN (WGAN):** Replaces the Jenson-Shannon Divergence (which struggles when distributions don't overlap) with the Earth Mover's (Wasserstein) Distance. It provides smooth, meaningful gradients even when the Discriminator is perfect, drastically improving convergence stability and preventing mode collapse.
* **CycleGAN:** Used for unpaired image-to-image translation (e.g., turning horses into zebras). It introduces a **Cycle Consistency Loss** ($x \to G(x) \to F(G(x)) \approx x$), ensuring that if you translate an image to a new domain and back, you get the original image.
* **BiGAN (Bidirectional GAN):** Traditional GANs cannot infer $z$ from $x$. BiGAN adds an Encoder $E$ that learns to map real images $x$ to latent space $z$. The Discriminator is trained to distinguish between joint pairs $(x, E(x))$ and $(G(z), z)$.

## 6. Exam Practice Questions & Solutions

### Q1. Computing GAN Loss (PYQ)
**Question:** In a GAN training batch, the Discriminator outputs the following probabilities for real samples: $D(x) = [0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.95]$. The Discriminator outputs the following probabilities for generated samples: $D(G(z)) = [0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0.4]$. Compute the discriminator loss $L_D$, the generator loss $L_G$ (using the theoretical minimax formula $\frac{1}{N}\sum \log(1-D(G(z)))$), and interpret the results. Use natural logarithms ($\ln$).

**Solution:**
**1. Discriminator Loss ($L_D$):**
The binary cross-entropy loss for the discriminator over a batch of size $N=4$ is:
$$L_D = -\frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 \ln(D(x_i)) - \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 \ln(1 - D(G(z_i)))$$

Real samples part:
$=- \frac{1}{4} [\ln(0.9) + \ln(0.8) + \ln(0.7) + \ln(0.95)]$
$=- \frac{1}{4} [-0.105 - 0.223 - 0.357 - 0.051] = -\frac{1}{4}[-0.736] = 0.184$

Fake samples part:
$=- \frac{1}{4} [\ln(1-0.2) + \ln(1-0.3) + \ln(1-0.1) + \ln(1-0.4)]$
$=- \frac{1}{4} [\ln(0.8) + \ln(0.7) + \ln(0.9) + \ln(0.6)]$
$=- \frac{1}{4} [-0.223 - 0.357 - 0.105 - 0.511] = -\frac{1}{4}[-1.196] = 0.299$

Total $L_D = 0.184 + 0.299 = 0.483$

**2. Generator Loss ($L_G$):**
Using the minimax formulation:
$$L_G = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 \ln(1 - D(G(z_i)))$$
Notice this is exactly the negative of the "fake samples part" calculated above!
$L_G = \frac{1}{4} [\ln(0.8) + \ln(0.7) + \ln(0.9) + \ln(0.6)]$
$L_G = \frac{1}{4} [-1.196] = -0.299$

**3. Interpretation:**
* The Discriminator's loss is quite low ($0.483$), meaning it is performing very well. It confidently assigns high probabilities to the real images (e.g., 0.9, 0.95) and correctly assigns low probabilities to the fake images (e.g., 0.1, 0.2).
* The Generator's loss indicates it is currently losing the minimax game. It is struggling to fool the Discriminator. Because the Discriminator is strong, the Generator's gradients might begin to vanish if this formulation is used, suggesting a need to switch to the non-saturating loss heuristic ($-\ln(D(G(z)))$) in practice to maintain healthy training.

### Q2. Generator Loss Progression (Homework)
**Question:** If the discriminator outputs for the fake samples change from $[0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0.4]$ to $[0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9]$, calculate the new generator loss and compare the two cases.

**Solution:**
New $L_G = \frac{1}{4} [\ln(1-0.6) + \ln(1-0.7) + \ln(1-0.8) + \ln(1-0.9)]$
New $L_G = \frac{1}{4} [\ln(0.4) + \ln(0.3) + \ln(0.2) + \ln(0.1)]$
New $L_G = \frac{1}{4} [-0.916 - 1.204 - 1.609 - 2.302]$
New $L_G = \frac{1}{4} [-6.031] = -1.508$

**Comparison:** 
The new generator loss ($-1.508$) is much more negative (lower) than the previous loss ($-0.299$). Since the Generator's objective in the minimax formulation is to *minimize* $L_G$, this lower value proves the Generator is performing significantly better! By forcing the Discriminator to output values closer to $1$ for fake images (e.g., $0.8, 0.9$), the Generator is successfully fooling the Discriminator into thinking its fakes are real.
