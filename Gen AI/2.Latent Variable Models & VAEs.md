# 2. Latent Variable Models & VAEs

Welcome to Module 2! This is arguably the most mathematically elegant part of Generative AI. We will explore how to discover hidden (latent) structures in data and how to generate new data using Variational Autoencoders (VAEs).

---

## 1. Autoencoders vs. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

### Vanilla Autoencoders (AEs)
A traditional Autoencoder consists of two parts:
1. **Encoder:** Compresses an input $x$ into a lower-dimensional latent representation $z$.
2. **Decoder:** Reconstructs the input $\hat{x}$ from the latent representation $z$.
* **Why they are NOT generative:** AEs are designed solely for **compression** and reconstruction. Their latent space $Z$ is not regularized. If you pick a random point $z$ from the latent space and pass it through the decoder, you will likely get garbage output. The model only knows how to decode the specific, exact points it mapped from the training data.

### Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
VAEs fix this by enforcing a strict structure on the latent space.
Instead of mapping an input to a single, deterministic point $z$, the VAE encoder maps the input to a **probability distribution** over the latent space.
* **Why they ARE generative:** Because we force the latent space to follow a known prior distribution (usually a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$), the latent space becomes continuous and smooth. We can easily sample a random noise vector $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and pass it to the decoder to generate a brand new, realistic image.

## 2. Variational Inference and the ELBO

In generative modeling, we assume our data $x$ is generated from some latent variables $z$. We want to find the true posterior $p(z|x)$ (i.e., what latent factors caused this image?).
By Bayes' Theorem:
$$
p(z|x) = \frac{p(x|z)p(z)}{p(x)}
$$
The denominator, $p(x) = \int p(x|z)p(z) dz$, is intractable (impossible to compute) because it requires integrating over all possible latent factors.

**Variational Inference (VI)** solves this by introducing a new, tractable distribution $q(z|x)$ (our Encoder network) to approximate the intractable true posterior $p(z|x)$.

### The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)
We want our approximation $q(z|x)$ to be as close to $p(z|x)$ as possible. We measure this using KL Divergence:
$$
D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x)) \ge 0
$$
Using Bayes' theorem and properties of logs, we can rearrange this into:
$$
\log p(x) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z)) + D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x))
$$
Because $D_{KL}(q || p) \ge 0$, we can drop it to create a strict lower bound on our log-likelihood $\log p(x)$:
$$
\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z))
$$

This right side is the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**!
* **Term 1 ($\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(x|z)]$):** The **Reconstruction Term**. It encourages the decoder to accurately reconstruct $x$ from $z$.
* **Term 2 ($- D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z))$):** The **Regularization Term**. It forces our encoder's distribution $q(z|x)$ to be close to our prior $p(z)$ (a standard normal distribution).

Because $\log p(x)$ is fixed for a given dataset, **minimizing the KL Divergence between our approximation and the true posterior ($D_{KL}(q || p)$) is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the ELBO.**

---

## 3. Why $D_{KL}(q || p)$ instead of $D_{KL}(p || q)$?

In Variational Inference, we use the **Reverse KL Divergence** $D_{KL}(q(z|x) || p(z|x))$ rather than the Forward KL Divergence $D_{KL}(p(z|x) || q(z|x))$. Why?

1. **Computational Feasibility:** Forward KL requires taking an expectation with respect to the true posterior $p(z|x)$: $\mathbb{E}_{p}[\log \frac{p}{q}]$. But $p(z|x)$ is exactly the intractable distribution we are trying to avoid! Reverse KL takes the expectation with respect to $q(z|x)$, which is our own neural network that we can easily sample from.
2. **Mode Seeking vs. Mean Seeking:** Reverse KL is "mode-seeking". It forces $q$ to fit tightly around one of the peaks (modes) of the true posterior, ensuring that samples drawn from $q$ are highly probable under $p$, even if it ignores other peaks.

## 4. The Reparameterization Trick

To train a VAE, we need to use backpropagation. The flow of data is:
$x \to$ Encoder $\to \mu, \sigma \to$ Sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \to$ Decoder $\to \hat{x}$

**The Problem:** The step where we sample $z$ is a stochastic (random) process. You **cannot compute the gradient** of a random sampling operation. Backpropagation stops dead at the sampling node, meaning the Encoder would never get updated!

**The Solution (Reparameterization Trick):** We separate the randomness from the network's parameters.
Instead of sampling $z$ directly from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, we:
1. Sample a random noise vector $\epsilon$ from a standard normal distribution: $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
2. Calculate $z$ deterministically using the formula:
   $$
   z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
   $$
*(where $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication)*

Now, the randomness is isolated in $\epsilon$, which doesn't depend on the network weights. The operations involving $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are just addition and multiplication, which are fully differentiable! Backpropagation can now flow through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to update the Encoder.

## 5. Exam Practice Questions & Solutions

### Q1. VAEs vs Autoencoders and the Reparameterization Trick
**Question:** Why are VAEs considered generative models, while vanilla autoencoders are not? What is the reparameterization trick in VAE, how and why is it implemented? Given $\mu = [0.5, -1.0]$, $\sigma = [0.2, 0.5]$ and a random sample $\epsilon = [0.1, -0.3]$ from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, compute the latent vector $z$ using the reparameterization trick.

**Solution:**
* **Generative vs Non-Generative:** Vanilla Autoencoders simply map inputs to discrete points in a latent space, leaving gaps. If you sample a random point from the latent space, the decoder will produce meaningless noise. VAEs enforce a continuous, structured probability distribution (usually Gaussian) over the latent space. This ensures that any randomly sampled point from that distribution will be decoded into a meaningful, realistic generation.
* **Reparameterization Trick (Why \& How):** It is implemented to allow gradient-based backpropagation through a stochastic sampling node. Directly sampling $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ breaks the computational graph. We implement it by moving the randomness to an independent variable $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and deterministically calculating $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$.
* **Numerical Calculation:**
  Given:
  $\mu = [0.5, -1.0]$
  $\sigma = [0.2, 0.5]$
  $\epsilon = [0.1, -0.3]$
  Using $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$:
  $z_1 = 0.5 + (0.2 \times 0.1) = 0.5 + 0.02 = 0.52$
  $z_2 = -1.0 + (0.5 \times -0.3) = -1.0 - 0.15 = -1.15$
  **Result:** $z = [0.52, -1.15]$

### Q2. Variational Inference Ellipse
**Question:** The figure illustrates the idea of variational inference (VI). The ellipse represents a family of approximate distributions $q(z; v)$ parameterized by $v$. The black dot outside the ellipse corresponds to the true posterior $p(z|x)$. Optimization starts from $v^{\text{init}}$ and converges to $v^*$.
A. Explain why the true posterior $p(z|x)$ generally lies outside the variational family $q(z; v)$.
B. Show how minimizing $KL(q(z; v) || p(z|x))$ is equivalent to maximizing the ELBO.
C. What does the path from $v^{\text{init}}$ to $v^*$ represent?

**Solution:**
* **A.** The true posterior $p(z|x)$ is often highly complex and intractable. The variational family $q(z;v)$ is intentionally chosen to be a simpler, tractable distribution (like a Gaussian). Therefore, the true complex distribution almost always falls outside the limited representational capacity of the simpler Gaussian family.
* **B.** We know that $\log p(x) = \text{ELBO} + D_{KL}(q(z;v) || p(z|x))$. Since the log-likelihood of the data ($\log p(x)$) is a fixed constant, any decrease in the KL divergence term must result in an exactly equal increase in the ELBO term. Thus, minimizing the KL distance to the true posterior is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the ELBO.
* **C.** The path from $v^{\text{init}}$ to $v^*$ represents the **optimization process** (usually via gradient descent). $v^{\text{init}}$ represents the randomly initialized weights of the Encoder network. $v^*$ represents the optimal weights where the distribution $q$ is as close as mathematically possible to the true posterior $p$, representing the highest achievable ELBO for this specific model architecture.

### Q3. Information Capacity and $\beta$-VAE
**Question:** A $\beta$-VAE has a reconstruction loss of 18.5 and a KL-divergence loss of 2.4. 
Given the objective: $\mathcal{L}_{\beta-VAE} = \mathcal{L}_{reconstruction} \pm \beta D_{KL}$ *(We will calculate using the standard loss formulation: Loss = Recon Loss + $\beta \times$ KL Loss).*
Calculate the objective value for $\beta=1$, $\beta=2$, $\beta=4$. Explain how increasing $\beta$ changes the relative importance of the KL-divergence term and discuss its effect on the information capacity of the latent representation.

**Solution:**
* **Calculations:**
  * For $\beta=1$: $\mathcal{L} = 18.5 + (1 \times 2.4) = 20.9$
  * For $\beta=2$: $\mathcal{L} = 18.5 + (2 \times 2.4) = 23.3$
  * For $\beta=4$: $\mathcal{L} = 18.5 + (4 \times 2.4) = 28.1$
  *(Note: If calculated precisely as an ELBO function with a minus sign $\mathcal{L} = 18.5 - 2.4\beta$, the values would be 16.1, 13.7, and 8.9 respectively).*
* **Explanation:**
  Increasing $\beta$ places a heavier penalty on the KL-Divergence term. This forces the encoder's learned distribution $q(z|x)$ to match the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ much more strictly. 
  * **Effect on Information Capacity:** Because the latent space is heavily regularized to look like random Gaussian noise, the model's ability to store specific, detailed information about the input image is restricted (reduced information capacity). 
  * **Effect on Disentanglement:** While reconstruction quality drops, this heavy bottleneck forces the network to learn only the most statistically independent, fundamental factors of variation, leading to a highly **disentangled** and interpretable latent space.
